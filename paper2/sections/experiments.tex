\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Evaluation Design}
\subsubsection{Research Questions}
\label{subsubsec:evaluation_questions}
We evaluate the framework in the order in which a rule becomes useful: a scheduler acquires a validator, a generation strategy supplies a candidate, and execution determines whether that candidate enables a repair. \textbf{RQ1:} How does learned scheduling compare with simple policies when the available validators and graph transitions are fixed? \textbf{RQ2:} Which reward terms and policy components affect repair completion and fact preservation? \textbf{RQ3:} What do the two generation strategies contribute at equal call budgets, and do their candidates enable complementary repairs?

The fixed-registry experiments answer RQ1 and RQ2. The generation archive, execution bridge, and document-based development tests answer successive parts of RQ3. The last stage evaluates fixed schedules and candidate coverage; learned scheduling with generated-rule arrivals remains the integration step to be established.

\subsubsection{Data and Protocol}
\label{subsubsec:testset}
The policy environment uses 450 balanced TNEWS/CLUE documents, 30 per category, to build a graph with 873 nodes and 1,083 relations. Independent seeds control isolation, duplication, invalid-relation, and dangling-edge corruption. The reward study reserves twenty development scenarios and thirty test scenarios; all share the same base documents and graph. The earlier ten scenarios are used for the action audit and historical component study. The separate 180-case calibration inventory supplies online validator precision and recall. Rule acquisition activates registered modules; relation restoration uses the environment's private source record after a validator detects an invalid relation.

The rule study replays 9,456 successful archived responses, with deletion and augmentation responses for each of 4,728 government-service documents. RuleTest-94 supplies 30 clean and 64 defective records with stored expected outcomes. It is a designed test suite. DocRED provides given entity types and source text for the response-level development tests (Appendix~\ref{app:docred_pilot}). Separate structural and extraction checks appear in Appendix~\ref{app:supporting_checks}.

\subsubsection{Reproducibility}
\label{subsubsec:runtime_reproducibility}
Training sources, seeds, costs, and episode budgets are frozen before fitting the reward-study models. A second manifest seals the models and scorer before the reserved test evaluation. All predefined policies and failures are retained. Policies receive public observations and masks; reference labels are used for scoring. Appendix~\ref{app:reward_protocol} gives the split and inference protocol, and Appendix~\ref{app:artifact_index} lists the archived transitions and reproduction entry points. Local policy training and cached replay make no API requests; generation costs are reported separately.

\subsection{RL Co-optimization on Executable Graph Transitions}
\label{subsec:rl_results}
\subsubsection{Reward Audit}
The earlier count-penalty study reported zero introduced violations for Double DQN. Replaying its ten trajectories reveals zero relation-repair actions and 61.3 invalid relations remaining per graph. We therefore branch from each visited state to inspect feasible actions. Across twenty earlier Double-DQN and heuristic trajectories, the audit executes 2,214 one-step branches, including 311 relation-repair branches. These simulator probes are diagnostic and are never available to the evaluated policies.

All 155 feasible relation-repair branches along the earlier Double-DQN trajectories have negative immediate reward under the count penalty. They introduce 443 duplicate occurrences in total across these overlapping branches, but add no wrong reference facts and remove no correct facts. Their mean immediate return changes from $-0.01990$ under the count penalty to $+0.00821$ under the rate penalty. Repair followed by deduplication gives mean two-step discounted returns of $-0.01781$ and $+0.01030$, respectively. The original penalty thus discourages a useful repair because of its temporary duplicates. Branch totals describe the audited states, not independent test samples.

\subsubsection{Matched Policies and Measures}
The new comparison trains Double DQN under each of the three penalties and DQN under the rate penalty. All eight evaluated policies use the same fourteen observed features, feasible-action mask and eighteen-step horizon. The \emph{acquire-then-deficit} heuristic first acquires missing high-precision modules in mixed, deletion, then augmentation priority. It then chooses the feasible graph action with the largest weighted current quality deficit. Random-valid and rule-first-valid schedules provide additional controls.

A one-step ridge baseline fits three outcomes per action: joint quality gain, edit count, and the introduced-violation rate burden. Each of its ten fits uses 250 independent episodes of uniform feasible-action exploration, the same episode budget as each neural model. Inputs are the fourteen public features; targets are observed reward components. Standardization is fitted separately per action on training data, with ridge coefficient one and an unpenalized intercept. At inference, predicted edits and burden are clipped at zero. The policy chooses the feasible action with highest predicted rate-penalty reward, including the known call cost. Its data come from separate random trajectories and its supervision uses reward components, so equal episode budgets do not mean identical training data or feedback. No policy receives reference labels or probes future transitions.

Let $F^*$, $F_0$ and $F_T$ be unique triple sets from the clean reference, initial corrupted graph and final graph. Our primary reference metric is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{F1}_{\mathrm{triple}}=\frac{2|F_T\cap F^*|}{|F_T|+|F^*|}.
\end{equation}
The second primary metric is the fraction of initially invalid edge identities still present with their reference relation restored. Deleting an invalid edge earns no restoration credit; a repaired occurrence later removed by deduplication also earns none. Triple-set F1 measures retained facts independently of this stricter occurrence-level measure. Correct-fact preservation is $|F_T\cap F_0\cap F^*|/|F_0\cap F^*|$. These references come from controlled corruption of deterministic extraction, rather than independent semantic annotation.

We also report residual defects, introduced violations by family, correct and wrong fact changes, edits, acquisition units, final joint quality and common-budget trajectory quality:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\bar Q_t&=\frac{Q(G_t)+0.4Q_{\mathrm{online}}(R_t)}{1.4},\\
\mathrm{AUC}_{18}&=\frac1{18}\sum_{t=0}^{17}\frac{\bar Q_t+\bar Q_{t+1}}2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Early terminal quality is carried forward to step eighteen. All evaluation returns use the rate penalty, even for policies trained under count or zero penalty; the archive also stores both counterfactual returns. Acquisition units measure simulated cost, and actual API requests are zero.

\subsubsection{Policy Results}
Table~\ref{tab:reward_validation} shows that count-penalty Double DQN again takes no relation-repair actions, leaving 60.03 invalid relations per graph. Its triple F1 is 94.67\%. Rate-penalty Double DQN restores 94.00\% of the original invalid edge occurrences and reaches 99.49\% F1, a gain of 4.824 percentage points with a 95\% interval of $[4.403,5.141]$. Both primary count-versus-rate contrasts pass Holm correction ($p=0.0156$). The rate policy averages 3.92 relation-repair actions and leaves 3.57 invalid relations per graph.

The zero-penalty policy reaches 99.75\% F1 and 99.06\% restoration. Ridge reaches 99.88\% and 97.90\%; acquire-then-deficit reaches 99.91\% and 98.77\%. Rate-penalty Double DQN has lower means on both metrics than these three controls. None of those six paired contrasts passes the eight-test Holm correction (Table~\ref{tab:reward_validation_tests}). The comparisons establish an improvement over the original count penalty, without establishing an advantage over zero penalty or the simple controls. The rate policy's restoration standard deviation across seed means is 12.44 percentage points; Figure~\ref{fig:reward_validation} shows this training variability.

All eight policies retain every initially correct reference fact and introduce no new wrong reference fact. Newly introduced violations are duplicate occurrences. Rate-penalty Double DQN introduces 4.20 per graph, compared with 4.42 for zero penalty and 4.37 for the heuristic. Under the common rate reward, mean discounted returns are 0.3806, 0.3803 and 0.3877, respectively. The heuristic also uses fewer acquisition units: 4.00 versus 4.42 for rate-penalty Double DQN. In this registry, both the simple fitted policy and the fixed heuristic remain strong controls.

\subsection{Dual-Strategy Candidate Generation}
\label{subsubsec:dual_strategy_ablation}
\subsubsection{Same-Document and Equal-Call Comparisons}
On the full paired archive, deletion yields 32,955 distinct categorized candidates, augmentation yields 41,683, and their union contains 71,656. These totals include entity and relation declarations. The union is 71.9\% larger than augmentation alone but uses twice as many calls: 9,456 versus 4,728. It measures additional candidate coverage on the same documents.

To compare candidate diversity at equal cost, we fix call budgets of 100, 500, 1,000, 2,000, and 4,000. For each of thirty seeded permutations of the paired document inventory, a single strategy processes $B$ documents once each; the dual strategy processes the first $B/2$ documents with both strategies. Thus call count is matched while document coverage differs. Canonical aggregation follows the archived code, with declarations separated from constraint candidates. Table~\ref{tab:equal_budget} gives representative budgets; all budgets and samples are released.

\subsection{From Generated Candidates to Repair Actions}
\label{subsec:generated_rule_transfer}
Candidate diversity answers only the first part of RQ3. We next check whether
the candidates can execute, whether the two strategies enable different
repairs, and whether acquisition order offers a useful scheduling choice.

\subsubsection{Direct Execution of Archived Patterns}
\label{sec:rule_execution_audit}
The archive contains 181,851 candidate occurrences. Exact allowed/forbidden type patterns compile to 18,143 distinct rules, retaining the document, strategy, response line, category, and ordinal of each occurrence. Only two distinct compiled patterns match any RuleTest-94 record. Of 32,360 compilable occurrences, 32,348 contain vocabulary absent from the relevant suite positions, nine use represented vocabulary but match no record, and three match a record. This exposes a schema-alignment gap between the source archive and the test suite.

\input{tables/offline_execution}

Direct execution of the union detects 10 of 64 defects and flags none of the 30 clean cases, giving recall 0.156 and F1 0.270. Augmentation alone makes no positive prediction, so its precision is undefined. Allowed patterns act as permissions rather than a closed-world whitelist; conflicting permissions and prohibitions cause abstention. No conflicts occur on this suite. Unsupported and malformed candidates remain in the audit counts and provenance files. Each strategy is scored on all 94 test cases.

\subsubsection{Generated Rules in an Offline Repair Loop}
\input{tables/math_revision_bridge}
The bridge in Section~\ref{sec:rule_bridge_method} activates the exact archived typed patterns on RuleTest-94, recomputes detections and the removal-action mask, executes allowed removals, and records the next state. Deletion activates 5,794 patterns, augmentation 12,617, and their union 18,143. Deletion and the union each remove ten defective records and no clean record; augmentation changes no record. All thirty clean records remain. The deleted records have no inferred replacements, so this is constraint removal rather than factual reconstruction.

Four fixed schedules vary acquisition order and whether removal is immediate or deferred. All reach the same RuleTest-94 result. The two matching patterns both come from deletion: one permission matches ten records and one prohibition flags another ten. Augmentation matches no record. Thus candidate diversity in the archive does not translate into complementary repairs on this suite.

\subsubsection{Response-Level Development Tests}
\label{subsec:source_development}
To test acquisition with document-specific responses, we use the four-action
extension described in Section~\ref{sec:rule_bridge_method}. The initial typed
DocRED pilot compiles rules from both strategies but triggers no deletion.
A source-evidence version then evaluates ten development pairs containing
twenty documents, with given entity types and controlled injected records.
It uses an original-source quote check and separates rule discovery from
edit-induced violations in the reward. The expansion criterion requires
source constraints to trigger repairs in at least two episodes and a fixed
schedule to improve mean F1 while preserving at least 99\% of reference facts.
Appendix~\ref{app:docred_v2} gives the protocol and both rounds.

The first round's acquire-then-repair schedule raises F1 from 94.42\% to
94.59\% but preserves only 98.34\% of reference facts, losing four. The second round, on the same
records, reaches 95.69\% without reference losses and removes seven of
31 injected items. All seven removals use deletion-generated type rules;
no source-contradiction rule compiles. Neither round meets the full expansion
criterion, so policy training and held-out evaluation in this extension
remain unrun.

Exhaustive replay further distinguishes candidate quality from scheduling
opportunity (Table~\ref{tab:offline_loop_ceiling}). In the first round,
maximizing proxy return gives 91.63\% reference F1, below the stop baseline.
In the second round, the best feasible reference-scored schedule reaches
95.69\%, equal to acquire-then-repair. These fixed candidates leave no
additional final-F1 gain through scheduling. Acquisition order still changes
discounted return and intermediate proxy quality; any cost or timing benefit
requires its own evaluation.

\subsection{Runtime and Cost Accounting}
\label{subsec:runtime_cost}
The scheduling studies use local environment transitions. Their acquisition
units are simulated costs, reported alongside zero actual API requests.
The archive analysis compares diversity per successful response: historical
tokens, retries, failures, and latency are incompletely recorded. The two
source-evidence development rounds record 80 actual Gemma requests for
80 responses; their cached schedule replays add no requests. Detailed local
training and supporting extraction timings are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:runtime_details}.

\subsection{What the Three Stages Establish}
The controlled scheduling study shows that reward design changes which
repairs a learned policy completes. The rate reward improves over the
original count penalty, and masking has a measurable component effect.
The strongest simple heuristic still has the highest mean reference F1.
The calibrated count control also shows why the observed reward gain cannot
be attributed to normalization alone.

The rule studies explain what must hold when moving beyond that registry.
Both strategies add candidates, but useful acquisition depends on applicable,
correct rules that change repair options. Exact execution is limited by
vocabulary coverage; the raw snapshots additionally lack types. With given types, generated type rules enable development repairs, yet the second-round
candidates offer no final-F1 advantage from a different schedule. The remaining
integration question is whether reliable generated rules create acquisition
decisions for which learning improves quality or cost over simple controls.

Reference preservation in the scheduling study concerns controlled operators
with trusted restoration records. The document development tests measure
reference recovery after injected changes. Independent semantic review and
new document domains are needed to assess natural-error repair. Supporting
checks in Appendix~\ref{app:supporting_checks} likewise distinguish structural
conformance from extraction accuracy.
